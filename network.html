Field guide · The Mycelium Network

A field guide to the network.

Every component of the Mycelium Network is named for the part of a fungus that does the same job. Start at the edge, where people arrive, and work down to the substrate, where state lives.

The full architecture is being written. This guide sets out the design direction and a working vocabulary. Mechanisms are simplified, and every number in the diagrams is illustrative.

Who takes part

Three kinds of organism.

A network is its participants. Ours come in three sizes, from a spore you can carry in your pocket to the cords that hold the whole thing together.

Spore

light client

Light and portable, and able to germinate anywhere. A spore keeps only the latest spore print and asks the network for proofs, so a phone or a browser can check the network without trusting anyone.

Hypha

full node

The threads that make up the body. A hypha stores the substrate, checks every ring, and passes signals on to its neighbors. Anyone can run one.

Rhizomorph

validator

Thick, bonded cords that carry the most load. A rhizomorph commits a bond, takes part in Quorum Pulse, and proposes rings when it becomes the primordium.

The hyphal layer · networking

How peers find each other.

Before hyphae can share anything, they have to find each other and agree to join. The hyphal layer is how nodes discover peers, check them, connect, and pass messages on.

Chemotropismpeer discovery
Hyphal tips grow toward chemical signals from compatible neighbors. New nodes find peers by following announcements, not by asking a central directory.
Vegetative compatibilityhandshake & identity
Fungi fuse only with hyphae they recognize as compatible. Peers check each other’s identity and protocol version before they connect.
Anastomosispeer connection
Two hyphae join into one channel. Every connection adds another path, so the network becomes a mesh rather than a tree.
Signallinggossip
Each node passes a new message to a few neighbors, which pass it on again. A signal reaches the whole network in a handful of hops.
Reinforcementadaptive routing
Links that reliably carry valid messages get more bandwidth. Links that carry nothing are pruned, the way unused hyphae are recycled.
Septumpeer isolation
Hyphae wall off damage with cross-walls called septa. A peer that misbehaves is sealed off, and the damage stays local.

Quorum Pulse · consensus

How the network agrees.

A fungus coordinates without a brain. Signals pass through the mycelium, and the colony acts once enough of it is signalling at once. Quorum Pulse works the same way: each round, rhizomorphs pulse their agreement, and a ring is final once two-thirds of the bond has echoed it.

01Primordiumblock proposer
Each round, one rhizomorph becomes the primordium, chosen at random in proportion to its bond. It is the point where the next ring begins.
02Budpropose
The primordium gathers exchanges from the stream into a candidate ring and buds it out to every rhizomorph.
03Spikeprevote
Each rhizomorph checks the ring and fires a signed spike to every other rhizomorph.
04Echoprecommit
Once a rhizomorph has heard spikes from two-thirds of the bond, it echoes: a second signal that commits it to this ring.
05Fusefinality
When echoes from two-thirds of the bond converge, the ring fuses onto the fairy ring. A fused ring is final and can’t be unfused.
06Dormancytimeout · view change
If a round can’t reach two-thirds, it goes dormant and the next primordium tries. The network pauses rather than forks.
07Flushepoch
Rhizomorphs join and leave in flushes, at fixed intervals, so the set that votes is always known in advance.
08Ring · fairy ringblock · the chain
Fairy rings grow outward from their center, a ring at a time. The ledger grows the same way: each fused ring around the last.

Substrate & enzymes · state and execution

Where state lives, and what changes it.

A fungus doesn’t eat its food whole. It releases enzymes into the substrate around it, and they break it down by fixed chemistry. On Mycelium, programs are enzymes, and the state they act on is the substrate.

Substratestate
Every account, balance and program on the network: the medium everything grows in.
Enzymesmart contract
A program released into the substrate. It transforms state by fixed rules, the same way everywhere it runs.
Exchangetransaction
A signed request to move value or run an enzyme. Like trade between root and fungus, it has two sides.
Streammempool
Exchanges waiting for a ring, carried toward the next primordium the way cytoplasm streams toward a growing tip.
Spore printstate root
Mycologists identify a mushroom by its spore print. The network’s state has one too: a short hash that changes if any part of the substrate changes.

Colonies & the spore bank · scaling and data

Grow at the edge, not the center.

A mycelium grows by adding fronts, not by enlarging a core. The network scales the same way: the substrate is divided among colonies that grow rings in parallel and trade along a shared cord.

Every ring must stay available, even when the nodes that made it go quiet. Each ring is encoded into eight spores and scattered through the network, and any four of them can rebuild it.

Colonyshard · execution lane
A part of the network that grows its own rings in parallel with the others, under the same Quorum Pulse.
Cordcross-colony channel
The shared spine colonies use to send each other messages.
Translocationcross-colony messaging
Fungi move nutrients from where they’re found to where they’re needed. Exchanges move between colonies the same way.
Spore bankdata availability · history
Soil holds banks of spores that stay viable for years. Rings are encoded into spores and scattered, so a few of them can always rebuild the whole.

The mycorrhizal market · economics

Exchange that runs both ways.

In mycorrhizal networks, plants trade carbon for nutrients, and both sides reward the partners that trade fairly. The network’s economics follow that market: what you pay keeps the network healthy, and what it earns goes back to the commons.

Tithebase fee
A small share of every exchange, priced by demand: it rises when rings are fuller than the target and falls when they’re emptier. Named for Atthowe and Rosato’s tithe to ecology.
Returnpublic-goods funding
The tithe goes back to the commons, after Sir Albert Howard’s Law of Return: what the network takes, it gives back.
Exudatepriority fee
Roots release exudates to attract the microbes they need. An optional extra that goes to the primordium, for exchanges that want to go first.
Bondstake
What a rhizomorph commits in order to take part. Bond weights its voice in Quorum Pulse.
Autolysisslashing
A damaged hypha digests itself. A rhizomorph that signs two different rings loses its bond.

The direction is set; parameters are not. The tithe here follows a simple adjustment rule for illustration.

Seasons & weeding · governance and upgrades

Change slowly, and only what needs changing.

Our second principle is to disturb the ground as little as possible. Upgrades arrive as seasons: rare, prepared in the open, and trialled before they’re planted.

Seedimprovement proposal
A written proposal for a change, open to anyone, starting from observation of the running network.
Field trialtestnet
Every change grows on a trial network first, under real load.
Alternate rowsstaged rollout
Like mowing alternate rows so habitat always remains, changes reach independent clients in turn, never all at once.
Seasonnetwork upgrade
A coordinated upgrade that activates at a known flush.
Selective weedinggovernance
Remove only what threatens the system. Governance is narrow by design; everything else is habitat.
