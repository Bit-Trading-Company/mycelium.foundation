Research

Open questions, worked on in the open.

The Mycelium Network is in research. That isn’t a disclaimer; it’s the phase where the important decisions get made. This is what we’re studying, how, and what we don’t yet know.

A working model

Networks that find their own shape.

In 2010, Tero and colleagues showed that a simple rule — tubes that carry flow thicken, tubes that don’t wither — lets a slime mold build a network as efficient and fault-tolerant as a rail system. The diagram below runs that rule.

A slime mold is not a fungus, but fungal networks follow the same principle: Bebber and colleagues (2007) found that a woodland fungus became more efficient and more robust as it grew by reinforcing and recycling its paths. Mycelium’s routing and fee design both start from this idea.

Research areas

Where we’re working.

Quorum Pulseconsensus
Safety and liveness under partial synchrony, proposer selection, and how fast a ring can fuse without giving up two-thirds agreement.
The hyphal layernetworking
Peer discovery that resists eclipse attacks, gossip that stays efficient as the network grows, and peer scoring that reinforces honest links.
The mycorrhizal marketeconomics
How the tithe should respond to bursty demand, how bonds should be sized, and how value returns to the commons without capture.
The spore bankdata availability
Erasure coding and sampling, so that spores can check that a ring is available without downloading it.
Coloniesscaling
Parallel execution and cross-colony messages that stay simple to reason about.
Specificationformal methods
A specification precise enough to be checked by machine, before it’s implemented by people.

Open questions

What we don’t know yet.

Good research starts by writing down the questions honestly. These are some of ours. If you’re working on any of them, we want to hear from you.

  1. Q1

    What is the shortest time to finality Quorum Pulse can reach while keeping its two-thirds guarantee, on real global networks rather than in simulation?

  2. Q2

    Can reinforcement-style peer scoring improve gossip efficiency without making the network easier to eclipse?

  3. Q3

    How should a demand-priced tithe behave when demand arrives in bursts, and what does “dynamic equilibrium” mean when it does?

  4. Q4

    How large should the bond be for a rhizomorph, so that participation stays open while equivocation stays expensive?

  5. Q5

    What is the simplest cross-colony messaging model that developers can reason about without surprises?

  6. Q6

    Where do biological analogies help network design, and where do they mislead? We want to know both.

How we work

Specification before code.

Open by default
Research, data and results are published openly, under licences that let anyone build on them.
Negative results count
An idea that didn’t work is still knowledge. We publish those too.
Reproducible
Simulations and benchmarks ship with the code and data to rerun them.
Honest analogies
Biology inspires the design; it doesn’t justify it. Every mechanism has to stand on its own engineering.
