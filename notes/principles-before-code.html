Principles before code

What an ecological farmer taught us about protocol design, and why we wrote our principles down first.

Before we write the network’s code, we wrote down its principles. Most of them come from farmers and biologists rather than engineers, and one farmer in particular: Helen Atthowe.

Atthowe has spent decades farming ecologically, as a horticulture extension agent in Montana, on Biodesign Farm, and with her late husband Carl Rosato at Woodleaf Farm. Her book, The Ecological Farm, describes a no-till, no-spray system that grows its own fertility. Her aim, in her own words, is to farm well by doing “less rather than more.” (She is not affiliated with the Foundation; we cite her because her work shaped ours.)

Pay attention first

The line of hers we return to most is “pay more attention and do less work.” For a protocol, that means measuring the running network before changing it. Every proposal should start from observation, not ambition.

Disturb the ground as little as possible

Tilling soil destroys the structure that makes it fertile. Changing a protocol’s core destroys the stability that makes it useful. So changes to the core should be rare, small and deliberate, and they should arrive as seasons: prepared in the open, trialled first, and rolled out in alternate rows.

Tithe to ecology

Atthowe and Rosato accepted a small share of crop damage as the price of a farm full of life, rather than spraying to eliminate every pest. They called it a tithe to ecology. We borrowed the word for the network’s base fee, and the idea for its design: budget for small, bounded inefficiencies in exchange for resilience.

Why write them down first

Protocols are full of trade-offs where both sides are reasonable. Without written principles, those disagreements get settled by whoever argues hardest. With them, we can ask which choice the principles favor, and show our reasoning. You can read all eight on the homepage.
