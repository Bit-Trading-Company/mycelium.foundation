Why everything is named after a fungus

Spores, hyphae, rhizomorphs and Quorum Pulse: why the network’s vocabulary comes from mycology, and where the analogy stops.

Every component of the Mycelium Network is named for the part of a fungus that does the same job. Light clients are spores. Full nodes are hyphae. Validators are rhizomorphs, the thick, root-like cords that carry the most load. Consensus is Quorum Pulse. Blocks are rings, and the chain is a fairy ring, growing outward one ring at a time.

It would be fair to ask whether this is just branding. It isn’t, though it is partly that. Here is why we did it.

Names carry a model

A name is a small theory of how a thing works. “Block” and “chain” describe a data structure. They tell you nothing about why the system holds together. “Rhizomorph” and “bond” describe a relationship: a cord that carries load because it has committed something. “Septum” tells you what happens to a misbehaving peer before you read the specification: it gets walled off, and the damage stays local.

When the vocabulary comes from a working biological network, it keeps reminding us of the properties we’re trying to reach: no center, many paths, reinforcement of what works, recycling of what doesn’t.

Where the analogy stops

Biology inspires the design. It doesn’t justify it. Every mechanism in the network has to stand on its own engineering: proofs, simulations and measurements. Quorum Pulse is named for the way fungi coordinate, but its safety comes from a two-thirds agreement rule that is well understood in distributed systems.

We also try to be honest about the science. Some popular claims about fungal networks, such as trees “talking” or “mother trees” nurturing their young through fungal links, are contested in the research literature. We use the parts that are well supported, and we say so when something is a metaphor.

A working vocabulary

The names are a working vocabulary, and some may change as the architecture is finalized. Every term has a conventional equivalent, listed side by side in the glossary, so nobody has to learn mycology to read the specification.
