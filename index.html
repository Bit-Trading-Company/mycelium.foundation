The Mycelium Foundation

Grown,
not built.

The Mycelium Foundation is a nonprofit that supports the Mycelium Network: a decentralized network designed to be fast, inexpensive and scalable, and to stay simple as it grows. We take our design cues from networks that have run beneath the soil for more than 400 million years.

Beneath the forest floor

Under every forest runs a network with no center.

Mycelium, the branching body of a fungus, threads through soil in every direction at once. It finds food, trades with plant roots and routes around damage, and it has no single point that can fail.

No brain makes those decisions. Each hyphal tip follows a few local rules, and the colony’s intelligence comes from everyone following them at once.

  1. -·

    Explore

    Tips grow outward in all directions, sampling the ground ahead.

  2. >+<

    Fuse

    When two threads meet, they join. A branching tree becomes a mesh, with more than one path between any two points.

  3. -=≡

    Reinforce

    Paths that carry the most flow grow thicker. The network learns its own best routes.

  4. :.

    Recycle

    Paths that carry nothing are broken down, and their material moves to where growth is happening.

In 2007, Bebber, Boddy, Fricker and colleagues found that a woodland fungus following rules like these became more efficient and more robust as it grew, while its relative cost fell. That is the property we want in a ledger.

The Mycelium Network

Designed like a network that already works.

The Mycelium Network is a decentralized ledger, currently in research. Our aim is to match the most advanced networks on speed, cost and scale, with a design that is easier to reason about and principles that are written down before the code.

Fast

The shortest healthy path.

Designed for quick finality. Messages travel the routes that are already carrying flow, the way nutrients move along a mycelium’s busiest cords.

Precedent · translocation along reinforced hyphae

Inexpensive

Nothing pays for idle paths.

A fungus doesn’t maintain threads that carry nothing. Users shouldn’t pay for capacity they don’t use either: low cost should come from the design, not from subsidies.

Precedent · recycling of unused hyphae

Scalable

Grow at the edge.

A mycelium grows by adding tips at its frontier, not by enlarging a center. Capacity should grow as participants join, without a core everyone has to pass through.

Precedent · apical growth and branching

Elegant

Few rules, everywhere.

Four local rules produce a whole organism. The fewer moving parts a protocol has, the fewer ways it can fail, and the easier it is for anyone to verify.

Precedent · colony behavior from local rules

These are design goals for a network in research, not benchmarks. We will publish measurements when there is something to measure.

Principles

Written down before the code.

Most of these come from farmers and biologists rather than engineers. When we disagree about the protocol, we’ll settle it by asking which choice the principles favor.

  1. I

    Pay attention first.

    “Pay more attention and do less work.” · Helen Atthowe

    Measure the running network before changing it. Every proposal starts from observation, not ambition.

  2. II

    Disturb the ground as little as possible.

    After Atthowe’s no-till practice and Masanobu Fukuoka

    Changes to the core protocol should be rare, small and deliberate. Stability compounds, like soil.

  3. III

    Keep a living root in the soil.

    Atthowe · keep roots growing year-round

    The base layer never stops. Liveness comes first, so everything built on it can depend on it.

  4. IV

    Diversity above and below ground.

    Atthowe’s first principle

    Many independent clients, operators and places. A monoculture fails all at once.

  5. V

    Recycle rather than import.

    Atthowe · Sir Albert Howard’s Law of Return

    The network should pay for its own upkeep from its own activity, and return value to the commons that sustains it.

  6. VI

    Reward fair exchange, in both directions.

    Kiers et al., Science, 2011

    Plants and fungi each reward the partners that trade most fairly, so control runs both ways. Incentives should too, so no participant can be captured by another.

  7. VII

    Weed selectively.

    Atthowe · selective weeding

    Governance removes only what threatens the system. Everything else is habitat.

  8. VIII

    Tithe to ecology.

    Helen Atthowe and Carl Rosato

    Atthowe and Rosato accepted a small share of crop damage as the price of a farm full of life. We will budget for small, bounded inefficiencies in exchange for resilience.

In the field · Helen Atthowe

A farm that grows its own fertility.

“Pay more attention and do less work.”

Helen Atthowe, ecological farmer

Helen Atthowe has farmed ecologically for decades. She spent seventeen years as a horticulture extension agent in Montana, ran Biodesign Farm there, and farmed Woodleaf Farm with her late husband Carl Rosato, an orchard first in California and then in eastern Oregon. She studied with Masanobu Fukuoka in Japan. Her book, The Ecological Farm (2023), describes a no-till, no-spray system that grows its own fertilizer.

Her aim, in her own words, is to farm well by doing “less rather than more.” Living mulches grow between the crop rows and are mowed in place to feed the soil. Alternate rows are left standing so beneficial insects always have a home. Roots are kept growing all year to feed the soil microbes, which she calls the engine of the whole system. She sums the approach up as “one simple concept: community.”

Her system, read as a protocol

Living mulch between the rows
An always-on base layer that protects what grows on it.
Mow alternate rows, so habitat remains
Upgrade in stages, so the network never loses its footing.
Grow your own carbon
Fund security from the network’s own activity.
Habitat for beneficial insects, close to the crop
Fund the researchers and auditors who keep exploits in check.
Weed selectively
Light-touch governance.
Dynamic equilibrium
Fees and issuance that settle around real demand.

Helen Atthowe is not affiliated with the Mycelium Foundation. We cite her work because it shaped ours.

The Foundation

We intend to be a nurse log.

In old forests, seedlings often grow from fallen trees. As fungi break the log down, it feeds what grows from it, and in time it disappears into the forest it nourished. That is the role we intend to play.

The Mycelium Foundation is a nonprofit. We support the Mycelium Network; we don’t own it, and we don’t control it. We are one participant in a larger community of researchers, builders and operators, and we’ll measure our success by how little the network needs us.

What we do

Research
Fund and publish open research into consensus, networking and incentive design.
Grants
Support independent teams building clients, tools and public goods.
Stewardship
Keep the specification clear, neutral and documented.
Education
Explain the network plainly, to anyone who wants to understand it.

What we are not

Not a company
We have no product to sell you.
Not the owner
The network belongs to the people who run it and build on it.
Not a kingmaker
We fund work, not winners.
Not permanent
If the network thrives without us, we’ve done our job.
